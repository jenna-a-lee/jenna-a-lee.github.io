<strong>Abstract:</strong> Phytoplankton blooms, especially diatom blooms, account for a large fraction of marine carbon fixation. Blooms vary greatly in frequency, intensity, and duration, lasting from days (irregular, short-term events) to months (canonical seasonal blooms). Species succession occurs as the bloom progresses, and high temporal resolution sampling is necessary to capture changes in community composition and their impacts on carbon and nitrogen assimilation rates. In order to assess these aspects of a bloom, we performed mesocosm bloom simulation experiments at the estuary-ocean interface of the Chesapeake Bay. Ambient dissolved nutrients, pigments, DNA, and 15NO3- and H13CO3- uptake rates (using tracer incubations) were all measured at least once per day over the course of the bloom. Rapid drawdown of nutrients and maximum carbon and nitrogen assimilation rates corresponded with peak chlorophyll a and fucoxanthin pigment concentrations. Fucoxanthin, typically associated with diatoms, was the dominant diagnostic pigment, with very low peridinin (dinoflagellate) and zeaxanthin (cyanobacteria) concentrations, indicating a diatom bloom. 18S rRNA gene analysis revealed high relative abundance of the dinoflagellate Karenia mikimotoi, a harmful algal bloom species which also uses fucoxanthin, during the mid- to late-bloom. K. mikimotoi did not, however, contribute significantly to the biomass, based on the dissolved nitrate:silicate drawdown ratio. Further 18S analysis showed that multiple species of diatoms co-occurred throughout the bloom, including during the peak. This challenges the traditional idea that the minimum phytoplankton diversity coincides with the peak of a bloom, in which one or two species dominate the biomass. Lastly, a co-occurrence network analysis showed that highly interconnected modules, despite being dominated by negative interactions between organisms, separated mainly by time (early vs. late in the bloom) and nitrate concentration.
